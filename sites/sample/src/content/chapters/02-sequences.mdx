A **sequence** is an ordered list of real numbers $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$.
Sequences are the foundation of analysis: limits, continuity, and integration all reduce,
at some level, to questions about convergence. Here is a citation: <Cite id="apostol1974" />.

## Convergent Sequences

<Definition id="def:convergence" title="Convergence">
A sequence $(a_n)$ **converges to $L \in \mathbb{R}$** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$
there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $n > N$ implies $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$.
We write $a_n \to L$ or $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$.
If no such $L$ exists the sequence **diverges**.
</Definition>

In symbols, the condition in <Ref id="def:convergence" /> is

$$ {#eq:conv-def}
\forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\; \exists\, N \in \mathbb{N} :\; n > N \implies |a_n - L| < \varepsilon.
$$

See <Ref id="fig:convergence" /> for a visual.

<Remark id="rem:Ndepends">
The integer $N$ from <Ref id="def:convergence" /> depends on $\varepsilon$: the smaller $\varepsilon$ is, the larger $N$
typically needs to be.  The definition says nothing about how $a_N$ itself behaves—only
the tail of the sequence matters.
</Remark>

<Theorem id="thm:limit-unique" title="Uniqueness of limits">
If $(a_n)$ converges, its limit is unique.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Suppose $a_n \to L$ and $a_n \to M$ with $L \neq M$.  Set $\varepsilon = |L-M|/2 > 0$.
Choose $N_1$ so that $n > N_1 \Rightarrow |a_n - L| < \varepsilon$, and $N_2$ so that
$n > N_2 \Rightarrow |a_n - M| < \varepsilon$.  For $n > \max(N_1, N_2)$:

<AnnotatedAlign rows={[
  {
    math: '|L - M| &= |(L - a_n) + (a_n - M)|',
    reason: 'Add and subtract $a_n$',
  },
  {
    math: '&\\leq |L - a_n| + |a_n - M|',
    reason: 'Triangle inequality',
  },
  {
    id: 'eq:limit-unique-bound',
    math: '&< \\varepsilon + \\varepsilon',
    reason: '$n > N_1$ gives $|a_n - L| < \\varepsilon$; $n > N_2$ gives $|a_n - M| < \\varepsilon$',
  },
  {
    math: '&= |L - M|,',
    reason: 'Definition of $\\varepsilon = |L-M|/2$, so $2\\varepsilon = |L-M|$',
  },
]} />

a contradiction.
</Proof>

<Theorem id="thm:squeeze" title="Squeeze Theorem">
If $a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and
$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} c_n = L$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = L$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $N$ large enough that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$,
$|c_n - L| < \varepsilon$, and $a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n$ all hold for $n > N$.  Then
$$
L - \varepsilon < a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n < L + \varepsilon,
$$
so $|b_n - L| < \varepsilon$.
</Proof>

## Cauchy Sequences

Convergence requires knowing the limit in advance.
Cauchy's criterion gives a purely intrinsic characterisation (compare <Ref id="eq:conv-def" />).

<Definition id="def:cauchy" title="Cauchy sequence">
A sequence $(a_n)$ is **Cauchy** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists
$N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $m, n > N$ implies $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$:
</Definition>

$$ {#eq:cauchy-crit}
\forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\; \exists\, N :\; m, n > N \implies |a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon.
$$

The equivalent formulation in terms of the sequence tail is:

$$
\begin{align}
|a_m - a_n| &< \varepsilon {#eq:cauchy-a} \\
|a_m - L| + |a_n - L| &\geq |a_m - a_n| {#eq:cauchy-b}
\end{align}
$$

where <Ref id="eq:cauchy-a" /> is the Cauchy condition and <Ref id="eq:cauchy-b" /> follows from the triangle inequality.

<Lemma id="lem:conv-cauchy">
Every convergent sequence is Cauchy.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Let $a_n \to L$ and $\varepsilon > 0$.  Choose $N$ so that $n > N \Rightarrow |a_n - L| < \varepsilon/2$.
Then for all $m, n > N$:
$$
|a_m - a_n| \leq |a_m - L| + |a_n - L| < \varepsilon.
$$
</Proof>

<Theorem id="thm:cauchy-complete" title="Completeness of $\mathbb{R}$">
Every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges.
</Theorem>

<Remark>
This property — every Cauchy sequence converges — is called **completeness**.
It fails in $\mathbb{Q}$: the decimal approximations $3, 3.1, 3.14, 3.141, \ldots$ form a
Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{Q}$ that converges to the irrational number $\pi$.<Footnote />
</Remark>

<FootnoteBody>
  The irrationality of $\pi$ was first proved by J.H. Lambert in 1761 using continued fractions. A more elementary proof via the integral
  $$
    \int_0^1 x^n(1-x)^n\,dx
  $$
  was given by Niven in 1947.
</FootnoteBody>

## Subsequences and Compactness

<Definition id="def:subsequence" title="Subsequence">
A **subsequence** of $(a_n)$ is a sequence $(a_{n_k})_{k=1}^\infty$ where the indices
$n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \cdots$ are strictly increasing.
</Definition>

Every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit (by <Ref id="thm:limit-unique" />), but divergent
sequences can have convergent subsequences—and in $\mathbb{R}$ they always do if bounded.

<Theorem id="thm:bolzano" title="Bolzano–Weierstrass">
Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ has a convergent subsequence.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $(a_n)$ satisfy $|a_n| \leq M$ for all $n$.  We use the **bisection method**:
start with $I_0 = [-M, M]$.  At each step, at least one of the two halves of $I_k$
contains infinitely many terms; call it $I_{k+1}$.  Since $|I_k| = 2M/2^k \to 0$,
the nested intersection $\bigcap_k I_k$ is a single point $L$.  Picking $a_{n_k} \in I_k$
gives $|a_{n_k} - L| \leq |I_k| \to 0$, so $a_{n_k} \to L$.
</Proof>

<Corollary id="cor:compact">
Every closed bounded subset $K \subset \mathbb{R}$ is **sequentially compact**: every
sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging to a point of $K$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
By Bolzano–Weierstrass any sequence in $K$ (which is bounded) has a convergent
subsequence.  The limit lies in $K$ because $K$ is closed.
</Proof>

We can now prove <Ref id="thm:cauchy-complete" />, which we stated earlier without proof.

<Proof for="thm:cauchy-complete">
Let $(a_n)$ be Cauchy.  First, every Cauchy sequence is bounded (choose $N$ so that
$|a_m - a_n| < 1$ for $m,n > N$; then $|a_n| \leq \max(|a_1|, \ldots, |a_N|, |a_{N+1}|+1)$).
By <Ref id="thm:bolzano" />, $(a_n)$ has a convergent subsequence $a_{n_k} \to L$.
For any $\varepsilon > 0$ choose $N$ so that $m,n > N \Rightarrow |a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon/2$,
and $K$ so that $k \geq K \Rightarrow |a_{n_k} - L| < \varepsilon/2$.  For $n > N$ and
$k \geq K$ with $n_k > N$:
$$
|a_n - L| \leq |a_n - a_{n_k}| + |a_{n_k} - L| < \varepsilon.
$$
Hence $a_n \to L$.
</Proof>

## Series

An infinite sum $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ is defined via the partial sums $S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k$:
the series **converges** when the sequence $(S_n)$ converges (in the sense of <Ref id="def:convergence" />).

The simplest example is the **geometric series** $\sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k$ with $|r| < 1$.
Its partial sum $S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n r^k$ has a closed form derived by a telescoping trick:

<AnnotatedAlign rows={[
  {
    id: 'eq:geo-subtract',
    math: 'S_n - r S_n &= (1 + r + \\cdots + r^n) - (r + r^2 + \\cdots + r^{n+1})',
    reason: 'Multiply $S_n$ by $r$ and subtract; write out both sums explicitly',
  },
  {
    id: 'eq:geo-telescope',
    math: '(1-r)\\,S_n &= 1 - r^{n+1}',
    reason: 'All intermediate powers $r, r^2, \\ldots, r^n$ cancel (telescoping)',
  },
  {
    id: 'eq:geo-formula',
    math: 'S_n &= \\dfrac{1 - r^{n+1}}{1 - r}',
    body: true,
  },
]}>
  <AnnotationBody row={2}>
    Rearrange <Ref id="eq:geo-telescope" />: divide both sides by $(1 - r)$,
    which is nonzero since $|r| < 1$ implies $r \neq 1$.
  </AnnotationBody>
</AnnotatedAlign>

Since $|r| < 1$ we have $r^{n+1} \to 0$, so taking $n \to \infty$ in <Ref id="eq:geo-formula" /> gives

$$ {#eq:geo-series}
\sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k = \frac{1}{1-r}, \qquad |r| < 1.
$$

<Definition id="def:series-conv" title="Absolute convergence">
A series $\sum a_k$ is **absolutely convergent** if $\sum |a_k|$ converges.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="thm:abs-conv" title="Absolute implies conditional convergence">
Every absolutely convergent series is convergent.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
If $\sum |a_k|$ converges, the partial sums $S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k$ form a Cauchy
sequence: for $m > n$,
$$
|S_m - S_n| = \left|\sum_{k=n+1}^m a_k\right| \leq \sum_{k=n+1}^m |a_k| \to 0
$$
as $n \to \infty$.  By <Ref id="thm:cauchy-complete" />, $(S_n)$ converges.
</Proof>

The most important test for absolute convergence (see <Ref id="def:series-conv" />) uses the ratio of consecutive terms.
Several standard tests are summarised in <Ref id="tab:conv-tests" /> below.

<Table id="tab:conv-tests" caption="Standard convergence tests for series $\sum a_n$">

| Test | Condition | Conclusion |
|------|-----------|------------|
| Ratio | $r = \lim \|a_{n+1}/a_n\| < 1$ | Converges absolutely |
| Ratio | $r > 1$ | Diverges |
| Root | $\limsup \|a_n\|^{1/n} < 1$ | Converges absolutely |
| Comparison | $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$, $\sum b_n < \infty$ | $\sum a_n$ converges |
| Alternating | $a_n \searrow 0$, signs alternate | Converges |

</Table>

$$ {#eq:ratio-test}
r = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|.
$$

<Theorem id="thm:ratio-test" title="Ratio Test">
Let $(a_n)$ be a sequence of nonzero terms and let $r$ be as in the formula above.
If $r < 1$ the series $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely; if $r > 1$ it diverges.
</Theorem>

<Remark>
The ratio test is inconclusive when $r = 1$.  The harmonic series
$\sum 1/n$ has ratio $r = 1$ yet diverges; $\sum 1/n^2$ also has $r = 1$ but converges.
</Remark>
