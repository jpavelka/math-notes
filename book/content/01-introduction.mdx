The central idea of analysis is that of a **limit**: the behaviour of a function near
a point, rather than at it.  Every major definition in this text — sequences
(<Ref id="def:convergence" />), derivatives (Chapter 3), and integrals — builds on
the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ framework introduced here.  Standard references are
<Cite id="rudin1976" /> (Chapters 4–5) and <Cite id="apostol1974" /> (Chapter 3).
This chapter covers limits (<Ref id="sec:limits" />), continuity (<Ref id="sec:continuity" />),
uniform continuity (<Ref id="sec:uniform" />), and the Intermediate Value Theorem (<Ref id="sec:ivt" />).

## Limits of Functions {/* sec:limits */}

<Definition id="def:limit" title="Limit of a function">
Let $f : D \to \mathbb{R}$ and let $x_0$ be a limit point of $D$.  We say
$\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$
such that

$$ {#eq:limit-def}
0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.
$$
</Definition>

<Remark id="rem:limit-deleted">
The strict inequality $0 < |x - x_0|$ excludes $x_0$ itself: the limit describes
behaviour *near* $x_0$, not at it, so a limit can exist at a point where $f$ is undefined.
</Remark>

The elementary arithmetic operations are compatible with limits.

<Theorem id="thm:limit-algebra" title="Algebra of limits">
If $\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to x_0} g(x) = M$, then
$\lim_{x \to x_0}(f + g)(x) = L + M$ and $\lim_{x \to x_0}(fg)(x) = LM$.
If additionally $M \neq 0$ then $\lim_{x \to x_0}(f/g)(x) = L/M$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
We verify the sum rule; the product and quotient rules are similar.
Let $\varepsilon > 0$.  Pick $\delta_1, \delta_2 > 0$ so that $0 < |x-x_0| < \delta_i$
gives $|f(x)-L| < \varepsilon/2$ and $|g(x)-M| < \varepsilon/2$ respectively.
Set $\delta = \min(\delta_1,\delta_2)$.  For $0 < |x-x_0| < \delta$:

<AnnotatedAlign rows={[
  {
    math: '|(f+g)(x)-(L+M)| &= |(f(x)-L)+(g(x)-M)|',
    reason: 'Rearrange the terms',
  },
  {
    math: '&\\leq |f(x)-L|+|g(x)-M|',
    reason: 'Triangle inequality ([ref:lem:triangle])',
  },
  {
    math: '&< \\tfrac{\\varepsilon}{2}+\\tfrac{\\varepsilon}{2} = \\varepsilon',
    reason: '$0<|x-x_0|<\\delta\\leq\\delta_1$ and $\\delta\\leq\\delta_2$',
  },
]} />
</Proof>

## Continuity {/* sec:continuity */}

<Definition id="def:continuous" title="Continuity at a point">
$f : D \to \mathbb{R}$ is **continuous at $x_0 \in D$** if $\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = f(x_0)$,
i.e. for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$ {#eq:cont-def}
|x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \varepsilon.
$$

$f$ is **continuous on $D$** if it is continuous at every point of $D$.
</Definition>

Comparing <Ref id="def:limit" /> with <Ref id="def:continuous" />: continuity at $x_0$
is exactly the statement that the limit equals the function value.
The condition $0 <$ in <Ref id="eq:limit-def" /> disappears in <Ref id="eq:cont-def" />
because $f(x_0) = \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$ forces the value at $x_0$ to be consistent with the limit.
See <Ref id="fig:continuity" /> for a geometric picture.

The operations that preserve continuity are summarised in <Ref id="tab:continuity-ops" />.

<Table id="tab:continuity-ops" caption="Operations preserving continuity of $f, g : D \to \mathbb{R}$.">

| Operation | Continuous? | Condition |
|-----------|------------|-----------|
| $f + g$,\ \ $f - g$ | Yes | — |
| $fg$ | Yes | — |
| $f / g$ | Yes | $g(x) \neq 0$ on $D$ |
| $g \circ f$ | Yes | $g$ continuous at $f(D)$ |
| $\|f\|$ | Yes | — |
| $\max(f,g)$,\ \ $\min(f,g)$ | Yes | — |

</Table>

<Lemma id="lem:composition">
If $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ and $g$ is continuous at $f(x_0)$, then $g \circ f$
is continuous at $x_0$.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Let $\varepsilon > 0$.  Since $g$ is continuous at $f(x_0)$, choose $\delta_1 > 0$
with $|y - f(x_0)| < \delta_1 \Rightarrow |g(y) - g(f(x_0))| < \varepsilon$.
Since $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, choose $\delta > 0$
with $|x - x_0| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \delta_1$.
Chaining gives $|x-x_0| < \delta \Rightarrow |(g \circ f)(x)-(g\circ f)(x_0)| < \varepsilon$.
</Proof>

<Theorem id="thm:composition" title="Composition of continuous functions">
The composition of two continuous functions is continuous.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Apply <Ref id="lem:composition" /> at every point of the domain.
</Proof>

<Corollary id="cor:poly">
Every polynomial is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
The identity $x \mapsto x$ is continuous; sums and products of continuous functions
are continuous (see <Ref id="tab:continuity-ops" />); every polynomial is built from
these operations.
</Proof>

## Uniform Continuity {/* sec:uniform */}

Uniform continuity strengthens the pointwise definition from <Ref id="sec:continuity" /> by demanding
a single $\delta$ that works everywhere on the domain simultaneously.

<Definition id="def:uniform" title="Uniform continuity">
$f : D \to \mathbb{R}$ is **uniformly continuous** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there
exists $\delta > 0$ — the *same* $\delta$ for all points — such that

$$
|x - y| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon \qquad \forall\, x, y \in D.
$$
</Definition>

<Remark id="rem:uniform-vs-pointwise">
Uniform continuity is strictly stronger than continuity: $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous
on $\mathbb{R}$ but not uniformly continuous, because for any fixed $\delta$ the
change $|f(x+\delta/2) - f(x)| = x\delta + \delta^2/4$ grows without bound as $x \to \infty$.
On a bounded domain the distinction disappears (see <Ref id="thm:compact-uniform" />).
</Remark>

<Theorem id="thm:compact-uniform" title="Continuous on compact implies uniformly continuous">
If $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ is uniformly
continuous on $[a, b]$.
</Theorem>

This result will be needed in the theory of Riemann integration.
Compare with the sequential compactness result from Chapter 2,
<Ref id="cor:compact" />.

## The Intermediate Value Theorem {/* sec:ivt */}

A continuous function cannot skip values.<Footnote />

<FootnoteBody>This topological property characterises connected sets: a subset $E \subset \mathbb{R}$ is connected if and only if every continuous $f : E \to \mathbb{R}$ has the intermediate value property.</FootnoteBody>

<Theorem id="thm:ivt" title="Intermediate Value Theorem">
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ and let $c$ be any value strictly between $f(a)$
and $f(b)$.  Then there exists $x_0 \in (a, b)$ such that $f(x_0) = c$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Assume $f(a) < c < f(b)$; the other case is symmetric.  Let
$$
S = \{x \in [a,b] : f(x) < c\}.
$$
$S$ contains $a$ and is bounded above by $b$, so $x_0 := \sup S$ exists
by <Ref id="thm:sup-exists" />.

By continuity of $f$ at $x_0$, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a
$\delta$-neighbourhood of $x_0$ on which $|f - f(x_0)| < \varepsilon$.
Points $x < x_0$ close to $x_0$ belong to $S$ (so $f(x) < c$), forcing
$f(x_0) \leq c$.  Points $x > x_0$ lie outside $S$ (so $f(x) \geq c$),
forcing $f(x_0) \geq c$.  Hence $f(x_0) = c$.
</Proof>

<Corollary id="cor:ivt-root" title="Existence of roots">
If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs,
then $f$ has at least one root in $(a, b)$.
</Corollary>

The next chapter develops an alternative characterisation of continuity:
$f$ is continuous at $x_0$ if and only if $f(x_n) \to f(x_0)$ for every sequence
$x_n \to x_0$ (cf. <Ref id="def:convergence" />).
