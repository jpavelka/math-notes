Differentiation formalises the notion of an instantaneous rate of change.
The key new idea — compared with the limits of Chapter 1 — is that we fix one
point $x_0$ and ask how quickly $f$ changes there.  The main results here
(the product rule, chain rule, and Mean Value Theorem) are proved using only
the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ machinery already developed; see <Cite id="rudin1976" />
(Chapter 5) for a comprehensive treatment.

## The Derivative

<Definition id="def:derivative" title="Derivative">
Let $f : (a,b) \to \mathbb{R}$ and $x_0 \in (a,b)$.  The **derivative of $f$ at $x_0$** is

$$ {#eq:derivative-def}
f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},
$$

provided the limit exists.  When it does, we say $f$ is **differentiable at $x_0$**.
</Definition>

<Remark id="rem:diff-notation">
Alternative notations: $\dfrac{df}{dx}\big|_{x_0}$, $Df(x_0)$, and $\dot{f}(x_0)$ (in
physics, for time derivatives) all denote the same object.
The limit in <Ref id="eq:derivative-def" /> is the slope of the tangent line at $(x_0, f(x_0))$,
obtained as the limiting slope of secant lines through $(x_0, f(x_0))$
and $(x_0+h, f(x_0+h))$ as $h \to 0$; see <Ref id="fig:derivative" /> and <Ref id="vid:derivative" />.
</Remark>

## Differentiability and Continuity

A differentiable function is automatically continuous, but the converse fails
(e.g. $|x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$).

<Theorem id="thm:diff-implies-cont" title="Differentiability implies continuity">
If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
For $x \neq x_0$, factor the difference as a product:

<AnnotatedAlign rows={[
  {
    math: 'f(x)-f(x_0) &= \\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\\cdot(x-x_0)',
    reason: 'Multiply and divide by $x-x_0$; valid since $x\\neq x_0$',
  },
  {
    math: '&\\to f\'(x_0)\\cdot 0 = 0 \\quad\\text{as }x\\to x_0',
    reason: 'Product of limits ([ref:thm:limit-algebra]): the difference quotient converges to $f\'(x_0)$ by [ref:def:derivative], and $(x-x_0)\\to 0$',
  },
]} />

Hence $\lim_{x\to x_0} f(x) = f(x_0)$, which is continuity at $x_0$
(see <Ref id="def:continuous" />).
</Proof>

## Differentiation Rules

The table below records the standard rules for differentiation of elementary functions.
All are proved by direct calculation from <Ref id="def:derivative" />.

<Table id="tab:diff-rules" caption="Standard differentiation rules.  Here $f$ and $g$ are differentiable and $n, c$ are constants.">

| Function | Derivative | Name |
|----------|-----------|------|
| $c$ | $0$ | Constant |
| $x^n$ | $nx^{n-1}$ | Power rule |
| $cf(x)$ | $cf'(x)$ | Scalar multiple |
| $f(x)+g(x)$ | $f'(x)+g'(x)$ | Sum rule |
| $f(x)g(x)$ | $f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)$ | Product rule |
| $f(x)/g(x)$ | $(f'g - fg')/{g^2}$ | Quotient rule |
| $(g \circ f)(x)$ | $g'(f(x))\cdot f'(x)$ | Chain rule |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ | — |
| $\ln x$ | $1/x$ | — |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | — |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | — |

</Table>

### The Product Rule

<Theorem id="thm:product-rule" title="Product rule">
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $x_0$, then so is $fg$, and

$$ {#eq:product-rule}
(fg)'(x_0) = f'(x_0)\,g(x_0) + f(x_0)\,g'(x_0).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
The standard "add and subtract" trick splits the limit into two recognisable pieces.

<AnnotatedAlign rows={[
  {
    math: "(fg)'(x_0) &= \\lim_{h\\to 0}\\frac{f(x_0+h)g(x_0+h)-f(x_0)g(x_0)}{h}",
    reason: 'Definition of derivative ([ref:def:derivative])',
  },
  {
    id: 'eq:product-split',
    math: '&= \\lim_{h\\to 0}\\left[f(x_0+h)\\frac{g(x_0+h)-g(x_0)}{h}+g(x_0)\\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}\\right]',
    reason: 'Add and subtract $f(x_0+h)g(x_0)$; factor: $g(x_0)$ is independent of $h$',
  },
  {
    id: 'eq:product-rule-result',
    math: "&= f(x_0)\\,g'(x_0)+g(x_0)\\,f'(x_0)",
    reason: 'Limits of both quotients exist ([ref:def:derivative]); $f(x_0+h)\\to f(x_0)$ since $f$ is continuous ([ref:thm:diff-implies-cont])',
  },
]} />
</Proof>

### The Chain Rule

The chain rule is the differentiation analogue of <Ref id="thm:composition" />.

<Theorem id="thm:chain-rule" title="Chain rule">
If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $g$ is differentiable at $f(x_0)$, then
$g \circ f$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and

$$ {#eq:chain-rule}
(g \circ f)'(x_0) = g'(f(x_0))\cdot f'(x_0).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Define the auxiliary function
$$
\varphi(y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{g(y)-g(f(x_0))}{y-f(x_0)} & y \neq f(x_0), \\[6pt] g'(f(x_0)) & y = f(x_0). \end{cases}
$$
By differentiability of $g$, $\varphi$ is continuous at $f(x_0)$.  For $h \neq 0$:
$$
\frac{(g\circ f)(x_0+h)-(g\circ f)(x_0)}{h}
= \varphi(f(x_0+h))\cdot\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}.
$$
As $h\to 0$, the right factor tends to $f'(x_0)$ and $f(x_0+h)\to f(x_0)$
(by <Ref id="thm:diff-implies-cont" />), so $\varphi(f(x_0+h))\to g'(f(x_0))$ by continuity.
</Proof>

## The Mean Value Theorem

The Mean Value Theorem (MVT) is the central tool for converting information about
derivatives into information about function values.

<Theorem id="thm:rolles" title="Rolle's Theorem">
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f(a) = f(b)$,
then there exists $c \in (a,b)$ with $f'(c) = 0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
If $f$ is constant then $f' \equiv 0$ and any $c$ works.  Otherwise $f$ attains a
value strictly above or below $f(a)$; by the extreme value theorem (which follows
from <Ref id="cor:compact" />) $f$ attains its maximum or minimum at some interior
point $c$.  At an interior extremum the derivative must be zero.
</Proof>

<Theorem id="thm:mvt" title="Mean Value Theorem">
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then there exists
$c \in (a,b)$ such that

$$ {#eq:mvt}
f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Apply <Ref id="thm:rolles" /> to the function

$$
h(x) = f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a),
$$

which is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and satisfies $h(a)=h(b)=0$.
</Proof>

<Corollary id="cor:mvt" title="Monotonicity test">
If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a,b)$ then $f$ is strictly increasing on $(a,b)$.
If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in (a,b)$ then $f$ is constant on $(a,b)$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
For any $x < y$ in $(a,b)$, the MVT gives $f(y)-f(x)=f'(c)(y-x)$ for some $c\in(x,y)$.
The sign of $f'(c)$ determines the sign of $f(y)-f(x)$.
</Proof>

## Taylor's Theorem

The MVT generalises to higher-order approximations via Taylor's theorem.
Define the **$n$-th Taylor polynomial** of $f$ at $x_0$ by

$$ {#eq:taylor-poly}
P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k.
$$

<Theorem id="thm:taylor" title="Taylor's Theorem with Lagrange Remainder">
If $f$ is $n$ times continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$ and $n+1$ times
differentiable on $(a,b)$, then for each $x \in [a,b]$ there exists $c$ between
$x_0$ and $x$ such that

$$ {#eq:taylor-remainder}
f(x) = P_n(x) + \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-x_0)^{n+1}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Remark>
When $n=0$, <Ref id="eq:taylor-remainder" /> reduces to the Mean Value Theorem
<Ref id="eq:mvt" />.  The remainder term quantifies the error in approximating $f$
by the polynomial $P_n$ and is essential for bounding truncation errors.
</Remark>

The convergence of partial sums $P_n(x)$ to $f(x)$ as $n\to\infty$ is the question
of convergence of a power series, which connects directly to the series convergence
tests in <Ref id="tab:conv-tests" /> (Chapter 2).
