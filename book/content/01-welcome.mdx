You're reading the class notes for _IMSE 884: Integer Programming and Combinatorial Optimization_.

## About the course

As the name suggests, the aim of this course is to give an overview of the related fields of integer programming and combinatorial optimization, which together comprise a large portion of the best-studied and most widely-applicable problems in optimization and mathematical programming. We will lean more heavily on the theoretical aspects of the subject, spending most of our time on the theory and algorithms that allow solutions to be found to these important problems. We will devote a relatively smaller amount of time to building and solving these types of models in practice.

As far as prerequisites, you should have some familiarity with linear algebra, at a minimum the basic mechanics of matrix math (addition/multiplication). Topics such as linear rank and determinants will surface as well. Some familiarity with other optimization topics, especially linear programming, will also prove helpful.

We will begin the class by exploring a selection of different combinatorial optimization problems and specialized algorithms for solving them. We'll then discuss preliminaries on integer programming, which we will see can itself be used as a tool for combinatorial optimization (as well as other types of problems).

In these discussions, we'll begin to notice how some problems seem to require more resources than others while modeling and solving them. This will motivate a discussion on computational complexity. We will not develop this topic completely formally, but at least at enough depth to help frame our discussions on what problems may be easier to solve than others.

From there, we'll take a deep dive into the theory of solving integer programs. We'll need to start with a review<Footnote/> of linear programming and polyhedra. This will allow us to discuss the main integer programming techniques of cutting planes and branch-and-bound. From there we will cover some more specialized solving techniques, and spend some time discussing practical matters of solving integer programs in real-life applications.

<FootnoteBody>
I expect that most of you taking this course have studied linear programming before. But don't worry too much if that's not the case, as we'll touch on all the theory we need during the review.
</FootnoteBody>

### Textbooks

The core of this course will be taught from these notes, thus students are not required to obtain a separate textbook. But I suggest anyone taking the course should grab the second edition of Laurence Wolsey's book _Integer Programming_ <Cite id="wolsey2020"/>. A large portion of the course's content is derived from this book (although we'll present things in a slightly different order), and you'll find further details there not covered in the notes.

There are a few other sources that I recommend, whose contents are woven into this course. I have to start with the book _Integer and Combinatorial Optimization_ <Cite id="nemWol2014"/>, written by Wolsey along with George Nemhauser, which is potentially _the_ reference for this subject<Footnote/>.

<FootnoteBody>
    Why didn't I choose <Cite id="nemWol2014"/> as the course text? I think the presentation and level of <Cite id="wolsey2020"/> is a little better suited for this course. It's also a bit newer, and although the theory found in <Cite id="nemWol2014"/> is evergreen, <Cite id="wolsey2020"/> contains a bit more updated information on techniques used in practice today.
</FootnoteBody>

Two books written by Alexander Schrijver, _Theory of Linear and Integer Programming_ <Cite id="schrijver1998theory"/> and _Combinatorial Optimization: Polyhedra and Efficiency_ <Cite id="schrijver2003combOpt"/>, are excellent resources and cover the subject at an impressive depth (though the writing style is rather terse, and can be a little challenging for readers that are new to the subject). On the less theoretical, more practical side is Williams' book _Model Building in Mathematical Programming_ <Cite id="williams2013model"/>, which gives several examples of taking real-world problems and modeling them to be solved as integer programs (as well as other types of optimization problems).

<Problem id="prob:maxWeightIndSet" title="Maximum Weight Independent Set Problem" label="MaxWtIndSet">
  <ProblemInstance>
    Graph $G=(V,E)$, non-negative integer vertex weights $w_v \geq 0$ for each $v \in V$.
  </ProblemInstance>
  <ProblemQuestion>
    Find a subset of the vertices $V' \subseteq V$ where no two vertices in $V'$ are adjacent, such that
    the total weight $\sum_{v \in V'} w_v$ is maximized. That is, find

    $$
    \argmax_{V' \subseteq V} \left\{ \sum_{v \in V'} w_v : (u,v) \notin E \; \forall \; u,v \in V' \right\}
    $$
  </ProblemQuestion>
</Problem>

Each circle represents a vertex in the graph, and a line drawn between two circles is an edge. There are two pieces of information given inside the vertices. First is the name of the vertex, some letter of the alphabet. Next is the weight associated with the vertex. So you can see vertex $a$ with weight 3, then vertex $b$ with weight 6, and so on.

The game is to create a so-called **independent set** of vertices:

<Definition id="def:independentSet" title='Independent Set' alt='Stable Set'>
  Given a graph $G=(V,E)$, an **independent set** is a subset of the vertices $V'\subseteq V$ such that no two vertices in $V'$ share an edge.
</Definition>

(If that $G=(V,E)$ language is unfamiliar, see <Ref id="sec:graphTheory"/>.) Importantly, your goal is to have the sum of the weights of vertices in $V'$ as high as possible.

For example, the set $\{b\}$ is an independent set with weight 6. The set $\{b, f\}$ is also an independent set, and a better one in the context of the game since the total weight of that set is 12 (6 for $b$ and 6 for $f$). The set $\{b,f,g\}$ has weight 15, but it doesn't count for our game since $f$ and $g$ are adjacent, and hence the set is not independent.

### Serious business {/* sec:combOptBusiness */}

Whether or not the above resonates with you, another reason to learn about these optimization problems is that they can be seriously useful in a business context, and knowledge of these techniques can be very useful on a résumé.

I know this from experience. In my career working in retail, I've built optimization models for improving efficiency in several aspects of online order fulfillment. For example:

- Determining what products are stocked in different warehouses, and in what quantities.
- When an order comes in from the website, determining from which warehouse(s) the order will be fulfilled.
- Determining which orders will be picked and shipped during each fulfillment "wave".
- Determining which size box to ship an order in.

All that for just online order fulfillment. There are several more examples in different parts of the retail industry, as well as nearly any other industry in the economy.

For other examples, we'll turn to some recent finalists for the [Edelman Award](https://www.informs.org/Recognizing-Excellence/INFORMS-Prizes/Franz-Edelman-Award), which is an annual prize given out by the professional society known as [INFORMS](https://www.informs.org) (the Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences). The stated goal of the prize is to "recognize excellence in the execution of operations research on the organizational level" and it highlights some great case studies of the use of optimization in companies worldwide. The awarded projects apply operations research principles to business problems; combinatorial optimization and integer programming use cases are often featured.
